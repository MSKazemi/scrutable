# Naming and identity questions

A good product name does not repair a weak product. It helps the right people recognize, remember, discuss, find, and trust a strong one. Naming should follow product strategy and user understanding, then pass linguistic, technical, and legal checks.

## Naming strategy

1. What exactly are we naming: a company, product, platform, service, edition, feature, protocol, or internal project?
2. Who must understand and use the name most often?
3. Who chooses, buys, recommends, administers, supports, or regulates the named product?
4. What problem, outcome, category, or worldview should the name evoke?
5. What is the product's clearest difference from its alternatives?
6. Which three to five attributes should people associate with it?
7. Which associations would be misleading, limiting, embarrassing, or harmful?
8. Should the name explain the product, suggest a benefit, create an image, or function as a distinctive invented mark?
9. What promise or expectation will the name create, and can the product consistently fulfill it?
10. What should someone remember after hearing the name once?

## Audience, language, and culture

11. In which countries, languages, scripts, dialects, and professional communities will the name be used?
12. Can intended users pronounce it confidently after reading it?
13. Can they spell it after hearing it?
14. Is it easily confused with another word, product, person, or organization?
15. Does it have unintended meanings, slang, political associations, insults, or awkward sounds in relevant languages?
16. Does pronunciation vary enough across languages to cause recognition problems?
17. Can screen readers and voice interfaces pronounce and recognize it reasonably?
18. Does the name exclude, stereotype, appropriate, or trivialize a group or culture?
19. Have native speakers and people from relevant cultures reviewed it in context?
20. Does transliteration preserve an acceptable sound and meaning where another script is required?

## Positioning and brand architecture

21. Does the name make the intended category clear enough, or will a descriptor be required?
22. Is it meaningfully different from names used by competitors and adjacent products?
23. Does it fit the organization's existing brand architecture?
24. Should it inherit the company name, stand alone, or become an umbrella for future products?
25. Can editions, modules, features, and plans be named coherently beneath it?
26. Will the name still fit if the audience, platform, geography, or feature set expands?
27. Is the name tied to a technology, trend, location, price, or feature that may become obsolete?
28. Does it work with a short, factual descriptor and tagline?
29. Does the verbal personality match the product: serious, friendly, technical, premium, playful, or authoritative?
30. Can sales, support, documentation, and users employ the name naturally in a sentence?

## Memorability and everyday use

31. Is the name short enough for its context without becoming cryptic?
32. Does it have a distinctive sound, rhythm, image, structure, or meaning?
33. Can a person recall it after a realistic delay without a list of options?
34. Can a person write or type it without explanation, unusual punctuation, or forced capitalization?
35. Does it avoid ambiguous characters, repeated letters, and easy misspellings where possible?
36. Are the initials or acronym clear, pronounceable, and free of harmful meanings?
37. Does it survive small-screen labels, navigation, notifications, URLs, commands, and conversation?
38. Can it be used comfortably as a noun, and as a verb only if that is intentional?
39. Does it remain recognizable in plain text without a logo or color?
40. Will people create an unwanted nickname or abbreviation, and does that matter?

## Discoverability and technical fit

41. Can people find the correct product when searching for the name and category together?
42. Is the name so generic that search engines, app stores, or users cannot distinguish it?
43. Are reasonable domain names available, affordable, and resistant to confusing variants?
44. Are relevant repository, package registry, app-store, social, and community identifiers available?
45. Does the name work as a safe URL slug, package name, command, file prefix, and identifier?
46. Could capitalization, punctuation, Unicode, pluralization, or word boundaries cause inconsistent use?
47. Does the name collide with common technical terminology, commands, dependencies, or file types?
48. Are likely misspellings or spoofing domains a security or phishing concern?
49. Can analytics, monitoring, and support searches distinguish this product from unrelated uses?
50. What canonical spelling, capitalization, pronunciation, and abbreviation will documentation enforce?

## Legal and reputational risk

51. Is the name already used by another product, company, open-source project, standard, or public figure?
52. In which trademark classes and jurisdictions would protection or clearance be needed?
53. Are identical or confusingly similar marks registered, pending, or commonly used?
54. Could similarity in sound, appearance, meaning, audience, or channel create confusion?
55. Who owns relevant domains, accounts, prior code names, artwork, and naming research?
56. Does the name imply certification, affiliation, geography, capability, safety, or results that cannot be substantiated?
57. Could the name violate a contract, platform rule, naming policy, or regulated terminology rule?
58. Has qualified legal counsel reviewed the finalists when commercial or reputational stakes justify it?
59. What is the cost and fallback plan if the name must change later?
60. Is the naming evidence and final approval recorded?

## Search visibility and collision audit

61. What appears on Google and Bing for the exact name in quotation marks in every important market and language?
62. What appears when the name is searched without quotation marks?
63. What appears for `name + product category`, `name + problem solved`, and the most likely remembered misspellings?
64. Is an older company, discontinued product, historical figure, famous brand, project, or acronym already strongly associated with the name?
65. Does an existing entity have enough links, history, coverage, users, or authority to dominate branded search results?
66. Are the first result pages relevant, neutral, negative, unsafe, or likely to confuse the intended audience?
67. Can the official website, repository, app listing, or profile realistically become a leading result for the exact name?
68. Do search engines interpret the candidate as a misspelling or variant of a more established name?
69. Are knowledge panels, maps, images, news, videos, app results, or shopping results dominated by another entity?
70. Is the exact or confusingly similar name used on GitHub, GitLab, npm, PyPI, container registries, app stores, social networks, and relevant industry directories?
71. Are useful domains and common misspellings available, or are they controlled by competitors, unrelated brands, domain sellers, or malicious actors?
72. Is the name associated with spam, malware, scams, scandals, adult content, extremism, or political controversy?
73. Can intended users distinguish and find the product when searching only from memory, without a supplied link?
74. Is the name distinctive enough to build and own branded search results over time without relying on a large advertising budget?
75. Has the team recorded each query, locale, date, leading result, link or screenshot, risk judgment, and final approval?

A keyword in a brand name does not automatically improve ordinary search ranking. For branded discoverability, prefer a distinctive name whose result space is not already controlled by a stronger entity. Search results vary by date, country, language, personalization, and engine, so preserve dated evidence and repeat the audit immediately before public adoption.

Repository, domain, package, account, and search-result availability are useful filters, but they are not trademark clearance or legal advice.

## Candidate generation questions

Use the [naming brief](../templates/naming-brief.md) before brainstorming.

1. Which words do users naturally use for the problem, outcome, and category?
2. Which metaphors express the product's role: guide, bridge, lens, workshop, shield, engine, map, or companion?
3. Which names can be made from a familiar word, compound, phrase, founder name, place, acronym, or invented sound?
4. What naming territories are strategically different rather than minor variations of one idea?
5. Can each candidate be explained in one sentence without inventing a complicated story?
6. What happens when we deliberately generate the opposite tone—formal versus playful, abstract versus descriptive, compact versus expressive?
7. Which candidates remain strong after removing logo concepts and visual styling?
8. Which candidates would a competitor be unlikely to choose?

## Recommended process

1. Complete the naming brief and agree on decision criteria before generating candidates.
2. Create three to five distinct naming territories based on the strategy.
3. Generate many candidates within every territory without judging too early.
4. Remove candidates with obvious relevance, language, usability, search, or collision problems.
5. Score a small set using the agreed weighted criteria.
6. Conduct linguistic, cultural, technical, domain, package, app-store, search-visibility, reputation, and preliminary trademark checks.
7. Test finalists with the intended audience using open recall, pronunciation, spelling, association, and comprehension tasks—not only preference voting.
8. Obtain appropriate legal review and record the final decision, evidence, risks, and fallback.
9. Define canonical spelling, pronunciation, descriptor, tagline, and usage examples.
10. Recheck availability immediately before public adoption because names and registrations change.

## Naming anti-patterns

- Choosing a name before agreeing on audience, positioning, and criteria.
- Selecting the option that receives the most internal votes without testing why.
- Asking users only “Which name do you like?”
- Copying current naming trends that make unrelated products sound identical.
- Using a deliberately misspelled name that must always be explained.
- Treating an available domain or repository slug as proof that a name is safe.
- Falling in love with a logo concept before the spoken and written name works.
- Hiding a weak or misleading name behind an elaborate origin story.
